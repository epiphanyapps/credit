## 1. FCRA - Fair Credit Reporting Act (15 USC 1681)

The FCRA is the primary federal law governing credit reporting. Three sections are critical for credit repair.

### Section 609 - Information Disclosure Rights (15 USC 1681g)

**What it does:** Requires credit reporting agencies (CRAs) to disclose to consumers all information in their file upon request.

**Key rights:**
- You can request a full copy of your credit file at any time
- CRAs must disclose the **original source** of every item of information, including any intermediary or vendor sources
- CRAs must identify every person/entity that pulled your credit in the prior 2 years (for employment) or 1 year (all other purposes)
- You are entitled to one free disclosure per year from each nationwide CRA under Section 612(a); additional copies may cost up to $15.50

**Why this matters for credit repair:** Section 609 is your investigative tool. Before you can dispute, you need to know exactly what is being reported, who reported it, and who furnished the data. If a CRA cannot identify the source of negative information, that is itself a basis for removal.

---

### Section 611 - Dispute Rights (15 USC 1681i)

**What it does:** Gives consumers the right to dispute any inaccurate, incomplete, or unverifiable information and forces CRAs to investigate.

**Key provisions (from the statute):**

| Requirement | Detail |
|---|---|
| Investigation deadline | **30 days** from receipt of dispute |
| Extended deadline | Up to **45 days** if consumer provides additional relevant information during the initial 30-day window |
| Cost to consumer | **Free** -- CRAs cannot charge for investigations |
| Dispute methods | Mail, online, or phone (certified mail recommended for paper trail) |
| No limit on disputes | You can dispute as many items as you want, as many times as needed |

**What the CRA must do upon receiving a dispute:**
1. Forward the dispute and all relevant information to the data furnisher
2. Conduct a "reasonable reinvestigation" to determine accuracy
3. Record the current status of the disputed information during the investigation

**If information CANNOT BE VERIFIED within 30 days:**
> The CRA must **"promptly delete that item of information from the file of the consumer, or modify that item of information, as appropriate."**

This is the core mechanism of credit repair. If a furnisher does not respond to the bureau's inquiry within the investigation window, the item must be deleted.

**After the investigation, the CRA must:**
- Provide written notice of results within **5 business days** of completing the reinvestigation
- Provide a revised credit report reflecting any changes
- Notify the consumer of their right to add a personal statement (up to ~100 words)
- Describe the investigation procedure used

**Anti-reinsertion protection:**
- Deleted information **cannot be reinserted** unless the furnisher certifies its completeness and accuracy
- If information is reinserted, the CRA must notify the consumer in writing within **5 business days**

**Practical leverage:** Credit bureaus often use an automated system called e-OSCAR that reduces complex disputes to 2-3 digit codes. This minimal processing may not constitute a "reasonable reinvestigation" as required by the statute, which can be a basis for further dispute or legal action.

---

### Section 623 - Furnisher Responsibilities (15 USC 1681s-2)

**What it does:** Places legal obligations on the entities (banks, lenders, collection agencies) that report information to credit bureaus.

**Furnisher obligations:**
- **Accuracy requirement:** Furnishers are prohibited from providing information they know or have reasonable cause to believe is inaccurate
- **Correction duty:** If a furnisher discovers information it provided is incomplete or inaccurate, it must promptly correct it with all CRAs that received it
- **Investigation duty:** When a furnisher receives notice of a consumer dispute (either from a CRA or directly from the consumer), it must:
  - Investigate the disputed information
  - Review all relevant information provided
  - Report results to the CRA
  - Correct or delete inaccurate information
  - Notify all CRAs that received the wrong data

**Direct dispute to furnisher (Section 623(a)(8)):**
Consumers can dispute directly with the original creditor/furnisher, bypassing the credit bureau entirely. This is called the "623 method" or "direct dispute."

**How to file a direct dispute:**
1. Draft a letter referencing Section 623 of the FCRA
2. Include: full name, address, account number, creditor name, description of inaccuracy, supporting evidence
3. Send via certified mail with return receipt
4. The furnisher has **30 days** to investigate and respond

**Strategic value:** This creates a second avenue of attack. You can dispute with the CRA (Section 611) AND directly with the furnisher (Section 623) simultaneously, creating dual pressure.

---

### FCRA Enforcement and Damages

If a CRA or furnisher violates the FCRA, consumers can sue and recover:

| Violation Type | Available Damages |
|---|---|
| **Negligent violation** | Actual damages + attorney's fees + court costs |
| **Willful violation** | Statutory damages of **$100 - $1,000 per violation** (no need to prove actual damages) + punitive damages + attorney's fees |

**Actual damages can include:**
- Out-of-pocket costs from disputing errors
- Lost wages from a rescinded job offer
- Higher interest rates paid due to inaccurate reporting
- Higher insurance premiums
- Denial of housing
- Emotional distress (with supporting evidence)

**Key legal precedent:** Multiple federal circuits (7th, 8th, 9th, 10th, 11th) have ruled that consumers do NOT need to prove actual damages to recover statutory damages for willful FCRA violations.

The FCRA is a **fee-shifting statute**, meaning the defendant pays the consumer's attorney's fees if the consumer wins. This makes it feasible to find attorneys who will take FCRA cases on contingency.

## 2. FDCPA - Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (15 USC 1692)

The FDCPA regulates third-party debt collectors (not original creditors). It intersects with credit repair primarily through debt validation and collection practices.

### Debt Validation Rights (Section 809 / 15 USC 1692g)

**The validation notice:** Within 5 days of first contacting you, a debt collector must provide:
- The debt amount
- The name of the current creditor
- A statement that you have **30 days** to dispute the debt
- A statement that if you don't dispute within 30 days, the debt is assumed valid
- An offer to provide the original creditor's name and address upon request

**Your 30-day validation window:**
- You have exactly **30 days** from receiving the notice to request validation in writing
- Upon receiving your written validation request, the collector **must cease all collection activity** until providing verification
- Verification must include: original creditor details, itemized charges, total amount owed, and documentation of the debt's legitimacy

**If the collector fails to validate:**
- They must stop all collection activity
- Continued collection without validation is an FDCPA violation
- You can file a complaint with the CFPB and your state attorney general
- You can sue for damages and attorney's fees under the FDCPA

**FDCPA damages:** Statutory damages up to **$1,000 per lawsuit** + actual damages + attorney's fees

### Pay-for-Delete Negotiations

**What it is:** An agreement where you pay (often a reduced amount) in exchange for the collector removing the negative tradeline from your credit report.

**Legal status:** Gray area. Pay-for-delete is not explicitly prohibited, but:
- The FCRA requires accurate reporting, and deleting a legitimate account technically violates this
- Most collectors are contractually bound by the credit bureaus to report accurately
- Collectors will rarely agree in writing due to their contracts with bureaus
- Newer scoring models (FICO 9/10, VantageScore 3.0+) already ignore paid collections, reducing the utility

**Practical considerations:**
- More likely to work with small, independent collection agencies than large ones
- Always get the agreement in writing before paying
- If offered verbally, follow up with written confirmation
- Original creditors almost never agree to pay-for-delete

### Statute of Limitations on Debt

**Two different clocks are running:**

| Clock | What It Governs | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| **Credit reporting limit** | How long negative info stays on your credit report | **7 years** from date of first delinquency (10 years for bankruptcies) |
| **Statute of limitations (SOL)** | How long a creditor can sue you to collect | **Varies by state: 3-10 years** |

**Critical rules:**
- The 7-year credit reporting clock starts from the **date of first delinquency** (the first missed payment that led to default) and CANNOT be reset
- The state SOL clock CAN be reset by making a payment or acknowledging the debt in writing (in many states)
- After the SOL expires, the debt is "time-barred" -- the collector cannot sue you
- Under the FDCPA (as amended), collectors are prohibited from suing or threatening to sue on time-barred debt
- Collectors CAN still contact you about time-barred debt; they just cannot threaten legal action
- Be cautious: requesting debt validation on near-expiry debt could potentially restart the SOL in some states if the letter acknowledges the debt

## 3. CROA - Credit Repair Organizations Act (15 USC 1679)

The CROA regulates companies that offer credit repair services. This matters both for understanding what legitimate credit repair looks like and for protecting consumers from scams.

### Prohibited Practices

Credit repair organizations **cannot:**
1. Make false or misleading statements to CRAs or creditors (or advise consumers to do so)
2. Alter a consumer's identity (e.g., obtaining a new SSN or CPN)
3. Guarantee specific credit score improvements
4. Charge fees before services are **fully performed** (the advance-fee ban)
5. Engage in any fraudulent or deceptive conduct
6. Include contract terms that waive the consumer's legal rights

### Required Disclosures

Before any contract is signed, the company must provide:
- A written disclosure informing the consumer that they can:
  - Obtain their own credit reports
  - Dispute errors themselves for free
  - Sue any credit repair organization that violates CROA
- A written contract that includes:
  - Complete description of services to be performed
  - Timeline for performance
  - Total cost / payment terms
  - Company name, address, and contact information
  - Cancellation policy

### Right to Cancel

Consumers have a **3-business-day cooling-off period** after signing to cancel without penalty or obligation.

### Enforcement

Consumers who are harmed by CROA violations can:
- Sue the company directly for actual damages
- Recover punitive damages
- Recover attorney's fees
- File complaints with the FTC and state attorney general

## 4. Actionable Strategy Summary

### The Dispute Playbook (in legal order of operations)

**Step 1: Obtain all three credit reports**
- Use AnnualCreditReport.com for free reports (Section 609/612 right)
- Review every account, balance, date, and status for inaccuracies

**Step 2: Dispute with CRAs (Section 611)**
- Send dispute letters via certified mail with return receipt to all three bureaus
- Identify the specific inaccuracy (wrong balance, wrong date, wrong status, account not yours, etc.)
- Include copies of supporting documents
- The CRA has **30 days** to investigate
- If unverified, the item **must be deleted**

**Step 3: Dispute directly with furnishers (Section 623)**
- Send a separate dispute letter directly to the creditor/furnisher
- Reference Section 623 of the FCRA
- Send via certified mail
- This creates a second investigation obligation on the furnisher

**Step 4: If dealing with collection accounts, send debt validation request (FDCPA Section 809)**
- Must be sent within **30 days** of first contact from collector
- Collector must cease collection until validation is provided
- If they cannot validate, the debt is effectively uncollectable and should be removed from your report

**Step 5: Escalate if needed**
- File CFPB complaint at consumerfinance.gov/complaint (wait at least 45 days after initial dispute)
- File complaint with state attorney general
- Consult an FCRA attorney (many work on contingency due to fee-shifting)

### Key Legal Deadlines to Track

| Action | Deadline |
|---|---|
| CRA must complete investigation | 30 days (up to 45 with new info) |
| CRA must notify you of results | 5 business days after investigation completes |
| FDCPA validation request window | 30 days from first collector contact |
| CROA right to cancel | 3 business days after signing contract |
| Credit reporting limit | 7 years from first delinquency |
| State SOL on debt | 3-10 years (varies by state) |

### What Triggers Mandatory Deletion

An item **must** be removed from your credit report if:
1. The CRA cannot verify it within 30 days of your dispute (Section 611)
2. The furnisher confirms the information is inaccurate (Section 623)
3. The furnisher fails to respond to the CRA's inquiry within the investigation period
4. The information is past the 7-year reporting limit (Section 605)
5. The information was the result of identity theft and proper procedures were followed
